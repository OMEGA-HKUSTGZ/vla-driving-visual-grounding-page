Visual Grounding in VLA Driving

Does Visual Information Play a Decisive Role in Vision-Language-Action Model Driving Behavior?

  • Jingtao He
  • Hongliang Lu
  • Xiaoyun Qiu
  • Yixuan Wang
  • Xinhu Zheng*
  1. Intelligent Transportation Thrust, The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou)
  2. *Corresponding author
Paper ↗ BibTeX
Overview Method Results Case study Citation

OVERVIEW

How much does a VLA driving model's behavior depend on what it sees?

Vision-language-action (VLA) driving models map image observations and auxiliary inputs directly to future trajectories, and are usually judged by aggregate metrics such as trajectory error, collision rate and rule compliance. Strong scores under clean inputs do not show how far the behavior is grounded in the visual input. This work introduces a structured framework that perturbs the visual input at three levels and measures how the driving behavior changes, under both open-loop trajectory prediction and interactive closed-loop safety evaluation. On a representative VLA driving model, the dependency differs across perturbation levels and between the two evaluation settings.

  • 01

    Three levels of perturbation

    Channel-level perturbations alter or remove the visual signal, information-level ones reduce its semantic density, and structure-level ones disturb its spatial organization.

  • 02

    Inference-time and model-agnostic

    Perturbations are applied during inference only, without updating parameters or retraining, and the formulation assumes nothing about the policy's architecture.

  • 03

    Open loop and closed loop

    The same perturbations are scored by trajectory error on nuScenes and by safety in the NeuroNCAP simulator, each as a relative change from the clean baseline.

METHOD

Perturb the visual input at three levels, measure the change in behavior

A VLA driving policy produces an action from a visual observation and auxiliary inputs such as ego-state signals and historical context. The framework replaces the visual representation with a perturbed one, in image space or in the encoder's token space depending on the level, keeps the auxiliary inputs unchanged, and re-evaluates the benchmark's own metric. It first computes the clean baseline, then iterates over the perturbations of each level.

  • Channel level

    Applied in image space. Gaussian replacement substitutes the image with a randomly generated Gaussian image of the same resolution and channels, removing its semantic content. Image removal omits the visual input, leaving a language-conditioned policy driven by ego-state and historical signals alone.

  • Information level

    Reduces information density at unchanged input resolution and encoder structure. Image downsampling scales the image down and back up to its original size before inference. Random pruning and FastV pruning discard 50%, 75% or 90% of the visual tokens before cross-modal fusion.

  • Structure level

    Rearranges visual tokens while preserving all their content. Global shuffling permutes the token features under unchanged positional indices; positional shuffling permutes the positional indices under unchanged features; block-wise shuffling moves blocks of spatially adjacent tokens (block-4 and block-8) as whole units.

  • Dependency measure

    For a perturbation T and benchmark metric M, D(T) = (Mperturbed − Mclean) / |Mclean|. It gives the direction and size of the change; its sign is read against the definition of the metric.

RESULTS

Open-loop error barely moves; closed-loop safety does

The framework is applied to Impromptu-VLA, in its publicly released 3B-parameter version built on Qwen2.5-VL. At each timestep the model receives one front-view RGB image and a fixed text prompt with ego-state signals and the history of the previous six timesteps (3 s). Open-loop evaluation uses nuScenes and reports the mean L2 displacement error at 1, 2 and 3 s. Closed-loop evaluation uses the NeuroNCAP simulation environment, with 30 runs per scenario, and reports the NCAP safety score with collision and out-of-bounds rates as diagnostics.

The no-perturbation row is the baseline shared by the three tables. A higher NCAP score indicates safer driving. In this paper's scoring, leaving the drivable area is additionally penalized as a collision failure, giving that episode a score of zero; the original NeuroNCAP protocol does not treat it as a failure category.

Channel level

Replacing the image with a Gaussian image raises the open-loop error by 3.9%, and removing the image altogether by 7.1%: even without image features the relative change stays below 10%. The paper reads this as offline trajectory estimation relying mainly on non-visual priors and learned motion patterns. In the closed loop the same two perturbations lower the NCAP score by 5.4% and 14.6%. The collision rate does not rise monotonically, but the overall safety score declines as the visual channel is progressively degraded.

Information level

Open-loop error varies only between 0.311 m and 0.329 m, at most a 5.8% relative change, even at 90% reduction. Closed-loop safety is far more sensitive: downsampling the image before the encoder lowers the NCAP score by 27.2% at 75% and by 31.5% at 90%. Pruning visual tokens after encoding costs less, with a largest drop of 17.4%. The paper takes the larger impact of pre-encoder degradation to mean that forming semantic representations matters more than keeping dense encoded tokens.

Structure level

Open-loop error rises by 1.9% to 4.5% under all four spatial reorganizations. In the closed loop the effect depends on scale. Global shuffling lowers the NCAP score by 7.0% and positional shuffling by 15.8%, so disturbing the spatial indexing hurts more than reordering the token content. Block-wise shuffling changes little: −1.1% for block-4 and +8.0% for block-8. The paper suggests the higher score under block-8 may reflect a shift toward more conservative behavior rather than better scene understanding.

Scope

The analysis is conducted on a single representative VLA model; the authors note that extending the framework to other architectures and training paradigms would further validate how general these observations are. They also state that the contrast does not invalidate open-loop evaluation, but suggests it gives only a partial view of interaction-critical visual dependency. As a possible explanation they point to the temporal consistency of historical cues in open-loop evaluation, whereas closed-loop scenarios require continuous perception–action feedback.

CASE STUDY

One closed-loop scenario under four visual conditions

The paper illustrates the pattern with a NeuroNCAP scenario in which a stationary vehicle is placed along the nominal ground-truth path. With full vision the model begins to decelerate after detecting the obstacle. Without visual input it does not react, and continues along a trajectory close to the ground-truth path of the original scenario. Random token pruning (75%) and global shuffling both still trigger deceleration; under pruning it is weaker, leaving the vehicle closer to the obstacle at the same timestep.

No real-world driving is shown or claimed on this page: the closed-loop results come from the NeuroNCAP simulation environment and the open-loop results from the nuScenes benchmark.
